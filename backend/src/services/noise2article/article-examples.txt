Title: So Anthropic and OpenAI Released Their Coding Models 15 Minutes apart. Here's Why it Actually Matter

Content:
Okay so last week was kind of wild.
On Wednesday, Anthropic was supposed to release Opus 4.6 at 10 AM. OpenAI was also releasing their new Codex model at 10 AM. Same time. Which, if you think about it, was already a bit ridiculous - like, did they coordinate this? Did someone leak something?
Anyway, Anthropic decided to just... release 15 minutes early. 9:45 AM. And then OpenAI stuck with their 10 AM time. So we got both "most advanced coding AI ever" announcements within a quarter hour of each other.
I was refreshing Hacker News and X trying to keep up. It was a mess. A fun mess, but a mess.
Here's the thing though - after spending the week actually using both of these, I have some thoughts that aren't just "wow benchmarks are high."
What Opus 4.6 actually does differently
The headline feature is "agent teams" - basically you can spin up multiple Claude instances that work on different parts of your project at the same time. One handles frontend, one does API stuff, another works on database migrations. They talk to each other and coordinate.
I tried this on a medium-sized codebase we have at work. Mixed results honestly.
When it works, it's genuinely cool. I had three agents working on different parts of a refactor, and they finished in maybe 20 minutes what would've taken me a couple hours. They even caught a conflict between two of their changes and resolved it without me doing anything.
When it doesn't work, it's annoying. Twice I had agents step on each other's changes in ways that required me to manually untangle things. Not a disaster, but not the "just let it run" experience Anthropic is selling.
The 1 million token context window is real though. I threw our entire codebase at it - like 50k lines - and asked it to find a race condition that had been bugging us for weeks. Found it in 12 minutes. Explained it clearly. Suggested three fixes. That alone was worth it.
GPT-5.3 Codex is faster, but...
OpenAI's new model is noticeably quicker for smaller tasks. Writing a utility function, fixing a bug, generating some boilerplate - it just feels snappier.
They made this big deal about how the model was "instrumental in creating itself" which... okay? I don't know what that means in practice. I couldn't tell any difference in how it debugs its own code compared to Opus.
My current setup: I use Opus when I need to work across multiple files or need it to understand a lot of context. Codex for quick stuff. Both for code review because why not, they catch different things sometimes.
The part nobody's really talking about
Two days before all this dropped, software stocks lost $285 billion in one day. Thomson Reuters, Legalzoom, a bunch of European companies - all down double digits.
Why? Anthropic released some legal automation tool and investors basically went "oh, these companies are going to get wrecked."
Meanwhile Anthropic's own Claude Code product hit $1 billion in annual revenue. Six months after launch.
So the companies making AI tools are making crazy money. The companies whose products AI might replace are watching their stock prices collapse. This is happening right now, not in some hypothetical future.
The "vibe working" thing
Scott White from Anthropic said something in a CNBC interview that stuck with me. He called it "vibe working" - like how "vibe coding" became a thing where you just describe what you want and AI builds it.
His point was that Opus 4.6 isn't really just for developers anymore. They're seeing product managers, financial analysts, all kinds of people using Claude Code because it's just good at doing tasks. Making spreadsheets, doing research, putting together presentations.
I don't know how I feel about this honestly. On one hand, cool, more people can do more things. On the other hand, what exactly is left for humans to do if AI handles the spreadsheet, the code review, AND the quarterly presentation?
The VCs being interviewed by TechCrunch this week were pretty blunt about it. One guy from Battery Ventures said 2026 is when AI goes "from making humans more productive to automating work itself."
That's... a lot.
What I'm actually doing with this stuff
My honest workflow right now:
For big tasks (refactoring, understanding a new codebase, finding bugs in complex systems) - Opus 4.6. The context window and sustained focus make a real difference.
For quick tasks (write this function, fix this error, generate this test) - Codex. Faster, just as accurate for small stuff.
For code review - both. Seriously. They catch different things. The extra API cost is worth it.
For the "agent teams" thing - still experimenting. It's promising but not reliable enough yet that I'd trust it to run unsupervised on anything important.
So what does this mean?
I don't know if either company "won" this week. Probably neither. The whole 15-minute drama will be forgotten in a month.
What won't be forgotten is that we're clearly in a new phase now. These aren't chatbots that help you write code. These are systems that can actually do substantial work across large codebases, coordinate with each other, and run for extended periods without constant hand-holding.
If you write code for a living, the question isn't whether to use this stuff. It's figuring out what parts of your job are still yours to do. The people I know who are doing well with AI are the ones who've figured out where their judgment actually matters - architecture decisions, understanding what users actually need, navigating team dynamics. The stuff AI can't see.
If you're building a product, I'd be thinking hard about whether your business can survive when capable coding agents cost basically nothing. A lot of SaaS is really just "we automated this workflow for you." Well, now users might just ask Claude to do the workflow directly.
Anyway, that's my read on the week. Still testing things, still figuring it out like everyone else.

Title: My Complete Guide to Managing OpenClaw Agent Teams
Over the past two weeks since using @openclaw (fka Clawdbot), I've been expanding my team of agents. In this article I'll walk through how I've set them up and the systems I use to manage them for maximum output.
Over the course of my career I've managed a lot of teams, both big and small, and my general approach with agents is to manage them the same way I'd manage human employees.
Turns out the hard part of AI isn't the intelligence, it's the management.
What you quickly learn about running multiple AI agents is that without structure, they're just expensive chaos. 
They will duplicate work, forget context, overwrite each other, make decisions that contradict previously agreed upon strategies, etc. It was painfully annoying at first.
It's basically the same problems that dysfunctional human teams have, and I've seen this firsthand so it was a problem I was confident in solving.
So I built a system that includes:
-Agent "hiring" and onboarding
-Leveling system
-Performance reviews
-Shared context
-Coordination protocols
Let's walk through each one.
Agent "Hiring" and Onboarding
The first step in the process is defining the role for each agent and building the SOUL.md files - this is where you store the important context about the agent, it's personality, it's skills, it's output and everything about them.
You DO NOT want to rush through the creation of the SOUL.md - it's like rushing through the hiring process IRL, if you hire the first candidate to apply you're not going to get top talent.
A couple simple rules for defining agents:
Be as specific as possible - for example, instead of a Research Analyst agent, you're better off defining it to a specific domain and type of analysis. Better - SaaS Equity Research Analyst. Now make the agent much more dialed into that specialty.
Be thorough - give the agent an origin story (get creative) and explain how this origin story shows up in their work, core philosophy (what is its north star guiding principles), inspirational anchors (who does it model its thinking after),  skills and methods, behavior rules, never dos, etc. Put in the work as if you were describing the absolute ideal person for this role.
Get feedback - once you've gotten your first draft, run it through a couple LLMs for feedback to make it even more robust
Once you are ready with the SOUL.md, you need a proper onboarding process. This includes the setup of the agent files (consistent with other agents), the access it needs, an announcement to the other agents so they all know this new agent exists, and inclusion into the workflow system. 
Just like companies have onboarding checklists, you need the same in your agent management system.
Agent Leveling Framework
When you hire a new employee IRL, you don't just give them root keys on day 1 - you loop them in as you gain trust over time. You should do the same with your agents.
I've setup a 4-level system for my agents and they each start off at level 1 and they get up-leveled through performance reviews.
Observer: can perform assigned tasks (e.g., research, writing, reviews, etc), but cannot take action
Advisor: can perform assigned tasks, recommend actions and execute on approval
Operator: can autonomously execute on assigned projects within guardrails (defined specifically) and reports out daily on progress
Autonomous: has full authority over permissioned domains (also subject to guardrails)
Trust is earned, not granted. 
Agent Performance Reviews
In some cases your new agents will crush it out of the gate, in other cases (i.e., a content writer for example) may take more time to find its groove.
This is where performance reviews come into play. I've just started this, but whenever I initiate a performance review, we go through a summary of the output and rate it, then based on that rating, make any decisions to up-level. Then feedback is passed to the agent for continual learning and improvement.
Agents can move both directions. I had a content agent at L3 who started rushing work. Quality dropped so I bumped it back to L2 for a week.
You need to treat it just like managing humans. 
Shared Context Changed Everything
One of my big breakthroughs was treating project context like a shared workspace with a central directory where any agent can read and update. When one agent learns something, it logs it so the next agent has full context.
This eliminated the cold starts when I initiated agents on projects. 
The key here is to develop a file structure for your context that makes sense. Each project has it's own folder with the following structure:
ACCESS.md - outlines which agents have access
CONTEXT.md - the working context for that project
research/ - the folder with all supporting documents
Any agent can read any project unless ACCESS.md denies them, then when an agent learns new context, they update CONTEXT.md. Context files have a "Last updated by" header I know who touched it.
They Coordinate With Each Other
I built an agent registry with skills and capabilities. When one agent needs help, there's a protocol: check who's available, provide context, hand off the task.
Last week I watched my design agent request help from a research agent for competitive analysis. Research agent delivered insights in 20 minutes. Design agent incorporated them and kept moving.
I didn't coordinate any of that.
Also, my agents built a web app that allows me to see an activity feed of everything going on, including which agents are active vs idle. This visibility gives me the confidence to let them do more work more autonomously.
The Key - Memory That Persists
My most annoying learning when I first started was agents forgetting things. I kept having to say "I've already told you this, go back through our chat logs and find it" but that didn't fix the core problem.
The real fix was a more robust memory system.
Every agent maintains three types of memory:
Daily notes (raw logs)
Long-term memory (curated insights)
Project-specific context (shared across agents)
It's backed up persistently. If I lose an agent and spin up a replacement, it has institutional memory from day one.
The Real Insight
AI agent management is the new workforce management.
What will separate top human performers from everyone else will be the ability to manage agentic workforces, and this will favor people who are more generalists, have prior management experience because they know how to coordinate output at scale, and those who are systems thinkers and can communicate well.
If you're building with AI, you're not just a developer anymore, you're a manager.
And most people are really bad at management so I hope this guide helps.

claude code just shipped agent teams. here's how to build your own marketing department inside it.
i've been using it on every major project and the results have been kind of ridiculous. here's how it works:
now, first of all - this isn't another prompt trick. it's a new way to run multiple claude instances that actually work together.
one lead coordinating, multiple teammates executing in parallel, all communicating with each other in real time.
it's experimental right now, and you have to enable it manually. but if you're running any kind of marketing operation, this changes the game.
here's what's actually happening under the hood:
you tell claude to create a team and define the roles. it spawns separate instances - each with their own context window, and they get to work. the lead coordinates. teammates claim tasks from a shared list. and unlike subagents (which just report back), teammates can message each other directly.
that last part is the unlock. they don't just work in parallel, they collaborate, challenge each other, and build on each other's findings.
here's how i'm using it for marketing:
campaign research sprint:
"create an agent team to research the launch strategy for [product]. spawn three teammates: one on competitor ad analysis, one on customer voice mining from reviews and reddit, one stress-testing our current positioning against what they find. have them share findings and challenge each other."
they work simultaneously. when the competitor researcher finds a gap, the positioning teammate pressure-tests whether we can actually own it. the voice-of-customer teammate validates whether real buyers even care. the lead synthesizes into a strategy doc.
landing page builds:
"create an agent team to build the landing page for [offer]. one teammate on copy and messaging, one on conversion structure and CRO, one running adversarial review as a skeptical buyer. require plan approval before any implementation."
the plan approval piece is key - each teammate has to outline their approach before executing. the lead reviews and either approves or sends back with feedback. catches bad directions before they waste cycles.
ad creative exploration:
"spawn 4 teammates to explore different hook angles for [product]. have them each develop one direction, then debate which is strongest. update findings doc with consensus."
the debate structure is the secret sauce. one agent exploring alone tends to anchor on the first decent idea. four agents actively trying to disprove each other? the angle that survives is actually battle-tested.
content production:
"create a team for this week's content. one teammate on search intent and competitive gaps, one drafting based on findings, one running the recursive quality loop on every piece before it ships."
parallel processing instead of sequential bottleneck. research and production happen at the same time, with built-in QA.
you get the idea.. these are just a couple of examples but imagination is the limit in terms of how far you want to take this.
now, here’s how to actually set agent swarm up inside claude code:
enable agent teams - add this to your settings.json:
{
"env": {
"CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_AGENT_TEAMS": "1"
}
}
tell claude to create a team in natural language. be specific about roles. "one teammate on X, one on Y, one playing devil's advocate" works. "make a team to help with marketing" doesn't.
use delegate mode if you want the lead to only coordinate. press Shift+Tab after starting the team. this prevents the lead from doing the work itself - it only spawns, assigns, and synthesizes.
require plan approval for high-stakes work. add "require plan approval before they make any changes" to your prompt. each teammate outlines their approach, the lead reviews, and only approved plans get executed.
talk to teammates directly when needed. Shift+Up/Down to select a teammate, then type. you can redirect anyone without going through the lead.
use the shared task list. the lead creates tasks, teammates claim them. you can check status anytime with Ctrl+T. if something's stuck, nudge the teammate or reassign.
let them argue. when you tell teammates to "challenge each other" or "debate," they actually do. don't flatten this - the friction is where the insight lives.
some mistakes to avoid:
don't use agent teams for simple tasks. single deliverables, quick copy tweaks, sequential work - just use one session. the coordination overhead isn't worth it.
don't let teammates edit the same files. two agents writing to the same doc = overwrites. break work so each owns different pieces.
don't spawn too many teammates. 3-5 is usually right. more than that and coordination overhead kills you.
don't let them run unattended too long. check in, redirect, synthesize as they go. the longer they run without input, the higher the risk of wasted work.
don't forget context. teammates don't inherit the lead's conversation history. include relevant context in your spawn prompts.
don't skip plan approval on complex work. it feels slower but catches bad directions before they burn cycles.
and a few things to keep in mind:
teammates each have their own context window. they load project context (CLAUDE.md, skills, MCP servers) but not the lead's conversation history.
token usage scales with team size. each teammate is a separate claude instance. worth it for complex work, overkill for simple tasks.
task dependencies work automatically. when a teammate completes a task that others depend on, blocked tasks unblock.
you can specify models. "use sonnet for each teammate" if you want to manage costs.
shutdown can be slow. teammates finish their current work before stopping.
clean up through the lead. when you're done, tell the lead to clean up. don't have teammates do it - can leave things in a broken state.
now, again, this isn't for everything. quick copy tweaks, single deliverables, sequential work - just use one session and keep it simple.
but for anything that benefits from parallel research, multiple perspectives, and built-in pressure-testing? agent teams compound fast.

A theory of the Post-Human Internet and the next form of commerce
The future of the internet isn't centered around human anymore.
That's not a metaphor. That's measurement. Cloudflare says it blocked 416 billion AI bot requests in just the second half of last year.
And that number is accelerating.
AI scrapers, RAG bots, autonomous agents, they're not just visiting the internet. They're starting to inhabit it. Browsing, querying, negotiating, transacting. Not on behalf of humans, but alongside them. Sometimes instead of them.
This is the beginning of something I've been calling the Post-Human Internet. And it breaks almost everything we assume about commerce, advertising, and markets.
The internet was built for one species: us.
Every pixel, every ad unit, every checkout flow, every recommendation engine all of it was designed around a single assumption: a human is looking at this.
A human with limited attention. Limited time. Limited cognition. A human who can be emotionally manipulated into clicking, buying, subscribing.
The entire digital economy, trillions of dollars, runs on this one bottleneck: human attention is scarce.
Impressions. Clicks. Conversions. Attribution. The whole machinery exists because you can only look at so many things.
But what happens when the thing looking at your ad isn't a person?
Agents don't have attention spans. They have compute budgets.
An AI agent can process 10,000 ads in a minute. Evaluate 1,000 products instantly. Check every review on every platform. Run cost comparisons across every vendor on earth.
An agent doesn't get tired. Doesn't get seduced by a lifestyle photo. Doesn't impulse-buy at 2am.
So here's the question that should keep every ad executive awake at night:
What does an "impression" mean when the viewer is an AI agent?
For a human, an impression is emotional exposure. Pixels in view. Time thresholds met. Memory and emotion engaged. The IAB literally defines it this way viewability standards are built around human eyes.
For an agent, an impression is computation. A parsable offer, evaluated against constraints, scored for outcome probability.
Human impressions stay expensive because attention is scarce.
Agent impressions become cheap because compute is abundant.
Example: An ad shown to a human: $10 of value. 
An ad shown to an agent: $1.
Not because the agent matters less. Because supply becomes infinite.
The entire pricing structure of digital advertising is about to be renegotiated.
You're already outsourcing your decisions. 
You just haven't noticed
Think about how you shop today.
You're overwhelmed. Too many products. Too many reviews. Too many brands running too many retargeting ads. Too many subscription options. The paradox of choice isn't a theory anymore , it's Tuesday.
Now AI gets good enough to handle it.
First, the small stuff. Your agent orders groceries. Picks your flight. Chooses the restaurant.
Then the big stuff. Furniture. Insurance. Cars. Investments. Healthcare.
You hand your agent your preferences, your constraints, your values, and a budget.
"Here's $1,000. Keep my life upgraded. Make good choices."
In that moment, something fundamental shifts. The agent becomes the buyer. The negotiator. The optimizer. The planner.
You keep veto power. But you lose day-to-day control.
McKinsey is already calling this "agentic commerce." Amazon is building shopping agents. Capgemini published a whole report on AI agents automating the path-to-purchase.
This is a transition already underway. And it means something profound for every brand on earth:
You're no longer selling to humans. You're selling to their agents.
Commerce today is a gamble. Simulation changes that.
You buy a couch. It looks great in the photos. It arrives. It's uncomfortable. Your cat shreds it in a week. It doesn't match your actual lifestyle at all.
This is how all commerce works. You purchase, then you experience the consequences. Expensive trial-and-error at every scale of life.
We've started chipping away at this. IKEA Place lets you preview furniture in AR. Virtual try-on is a billion-dollar category. 3D showrooms exist.
But these are shallow simulations. They only show you how something looks. Not how it lives. Now imagine an agent that doesn't ask "Does this couch look good in your room?"
It asks: "How does this couch perform in a three-month simulation of your life?"
Wear and tear. Pet interactions. Cleaning effort. Lifestyle compatibility. Regret probability. Emotional satisfaction over time. Durability curves.
The agent doesn't buy a product. It buys a future. It fast-forwards through the ownership experience before you spend a dollar.
This is what I call time-compressed lived experience.
And it's the foundation of a completely new kind of commerce.
Instance Commerce.
Not "instant" commerce. Instance commerce.
Here's the concept:
An "instance" is a simulation-ready bundle — products, services, configurations, lifestyle implications, all packaged as a scenario you can test-drive in simulation before committing to reality.
Example: IKEA sells a "Living Room Instance."
Couch. Table. Lamp. Carpet. Shelving. Wall decor. Arrangement blueprint. Everything configured as a coherent lifestyle scenario.
In the real world, that bundle costs $20,000.
The simulation instance? $20.
The agent exposure to that instance? $1.
This is economically logical. The marginal cost of simulating a lifestyle bundle is near-zero once the model exists. Brands produce instances at scale. Agents consume them at scale. The cheaper simulation gets, the more simulations agents run, the better the outcomes they find, the more humans trust delegation.
It's a flywheel.
So instead of you browsing Amazon for three hours, your agent runs:
1,000 furniture simulations
10,000 layout combinations
50 lifestyle bundles
5 long-term outcome forecasts
Then it comes back to you with three optimized futures and the reasoning behind each.

You don't browse products. You choose between possible lives.
Advertising stops being persuasion. It becomes proof.
Human advertising works because humans are emotional. Desire. Status. Envy. Fear. Aspiration. Impulse. The entire industry is built on triggering feelings that bypass rational evaluation.
Agents don't work like that.
An agent evaluates compatibility. Constraints. Long-term cost. Satisfaction probability. Regret risk. Alignment with your actual stated values.
It asks: "Will this product genuinely improve this person's life? Is this bundle stable under their constraints? What's the failure probability?"
So the ad funnel inverts.
Old funnel: Impression → Click → Purchase → Retention
New funnel: Agent Exposure → Simulation Purchase → Outcome Score → Human Approval → Real Purchase
This isn't a tweak. It's a different engine entirely.
Brands can no longer win by being the loudest, prettiest, or most emotionally manipulative. They win by having the highest simulation approval score.
The new advertising isn't about capturing attention. It's about surviving scrutiny.
The Agentic Free Market.
Adam Smith's free market was built on human limitations. Imperfect information. Slow processing. Emotional bias. Coordination through price signals.
Agents blow all of that up.
Perfect memory. Constant monitoring. Infinite comparison. Rapid negotiation. Algorithmic rationality.
If every human has an agent, and every agent has a delegated budget, you get a market that operates at machine speed. Supply chains adapt in real-time. Pricing becomes dynamic. Demand is forecast instantly. Negotiation is automated. Competition is continuous.
We've actually seen a preview of this already — in finance.
High-frequency trading turned financial markets into machine-speed arenas. It created new dynamics: flash crashes, algorithmic arms races, regulatory scrambles. Nasdaq literally shut down a high-speed trading service under regulatory pressure.
That was one sector. Now imagine it across all commerce.
Agent-based computational economics has been an academic discipline for decades — modeling economies as systems of interacting agents. The difference now is that we're moving from simulated agent economies to real ones. Actual autonomous systems participating in actual markets.
And agents won't just trade products. They'll trade compute. Access rights. Data streams. Simulation environments. Microservices. Digital labor.
An entire economy of automated entities negotiating value.
Brands stop selling products. They sell futures
This is the deepest disruption.
In instance commerce, a brand doesn't sell you a couch. It sells you:
"The minimalist apartment future"
"The cozy Scandinavian living room future"
"The high-performance remote work future"
"The pet-proof home future"
The unit of commerce becomes a packaged possible world.
This is the shift from product economy to scenario economy.
And brands compete by making their scenarios score higher. Lower regret rates. Higher lifestyle compatibility. Better long-term satisfaction. More durability. Fewer failure modes.
The new KPI isn't clicks.
The KPI is simulation approval score.
The questions that keep me up.
Who owns the simulation layer?
The dominant players in this economy aren't the brands. They're the simulation engines. Whoever controls the physics models, the behavior models, the preference models, the environment models, they control the flow of commerce. Not the power to show ads. The power to render futures.
What happens to human desire?
Humans desire irrationally. That irrationality drives culture, trends, identity, art. If agents filter out irrationality before it reaches the market, does commerce become more efficient but less human?
Who defines "best"?
If your agent optimizes for efficiency, safety, cost  but you actually wanted novelty, chaos, experimentation then the agent becomes a gatekeeper, not a servant. Value encoding: translating human contradictions into agent objectives might be the most consequential design problem of the next decade.
What is ownership when simulation comes first?
  If the simulation is more valuable than the object  if the first "product" is not the sofa but the sofa experience  then we face a deep inversion of the material economy.
You don't own things. You own access to the best simulations of things.
The bottom line.
We are approaching a world where the internet is not a human space. It is a digital habitat.
Agents are not tools living inside the internet. They are becoming native organisms of the internet. They have needs compute, data, bandwidth, incentives, survival pressure. They interact with each other. They form something that looks less like software and more like an ecology.
Once commerce, advertising, and market participation shift from humans to agents, the entire economic foundation of the modern internet reconfigures.
Commerce becomes a multiverse exploration problem. Agents explore a million possible futures and return with the best one.
In that world, humans don't browse product catalogs.
Humans browse possible lives.
This is Instance Commerce. And it's already starting.
